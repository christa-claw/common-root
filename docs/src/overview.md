## What it is

Common Root? is a free, open reader for exploring the scriptures of the Abrahamic
faiths — Bible translations, the Quran, Hadith, and commentaries — side by side,
in any language, at any depth.

The name is an invitation. Read these texts alongside one another and weigh for
yourself what they share and where they diverge.

The platform is independent. It is not affiliated with any religious organisation
or publisher. Reading and study are always free; an optional free account adds
a private study layer — notes, highlights, saved searches and your own
reading preferences — and a way to contribute.

## Who it is for

Scholars, students, and curious readers alike. Whether you are comparing how a
single verse is rendered across translations, tracing a prophecy across both
testaments, or reading a book as continuous prose the way its earliest audience
did, the reader is built to get out of your way.

## How reading works

The reader is built around **columns**. Each column shows one text — a Bible
translation, the Quran, a commentary, or your own notes. You can open as many
columns as you like and mix traditions freely. Columns scroll together by
default so the same passage stays aligned, and any column can be unlinked to
browse independently.

### Display modes

A core idea of the platform is that the divisions we take for granted —
chapters, verses, section headings — are editorial layers added long after the
texts were written. Each column can be switched between modes that reveal or
hide these layers:

- **Scriptio Continua** — continuous text in the ancient manuscript style, with
  no chapter or verse divisions. Chapters within a book run together; a labelled
  rule marks each book boundary.
- **Chapters (1227)** — the chapter system added by Stephen Langton around 1227.
- **Verses (1551)** — verse numbers added by Robert Estienne in 1551.
- **Titles** — modern editorial section headings, where the translation supplies
  them.

### Reading order

Each column can also present its books in different orders: the traditional
**canonical** order, or a scholarly **chronological** order that interleaves
passages across books — so the prophets and psalms can be read in their
historical setting rather than in isolation.

### Reading the Quran

A Quran column shows the Arabic text, and you can choose a translation to show
beneath each ayah. The Arabic stays primary, with the translation paired under it
in the same scrolling column so the two never drift apart. The translation picker
offers only the translations of that Quran.

### Lineage rungs

No translation is made from nothing. The World English Bible revises the American
Standard Version, which revises the Revised Version, which revises the King
James Version, which drew on the Geneva Bible and, behind it, the Greek and the
Hebrew. The reader makes that ladder visible, one rung at a time.

In **Verses** or **Titles** mode, two chevrons in the column header step along
the ladder: **▾** opens one more rung, **▴** closes one. An open rung appears
under every verse as a muted line, labelled with the edition's abbreviation and
year (`ASV 1901`, `KJV 1611`, `WLC 1008`), so earlier wording can be compared
verse by verse.

- **Only recorded parents.** A text is shown as a parent only where the revision
  or derivation is well attested. Where an edition's real source is not in the
  collection, the chain stops there rather than inventing a link.
- **Every Bible reaches the original languages.** The last rungs are always the
  Hebrew (WLC) and the Greek (TR). Where these are not a translation's documented
  source they are marked **≈** as *witnesses*: `≈ TR` under an NIV verse means
  "a witness to the Greek", not "the text the NIV was translated from".
- **Up as well as down.** Once the older rungs are closed, **▴** carries on into
  newer translations made from the one you are reading. These are always
  recorded descendants, never guesses.
- **Rungs follow the book.** A rung with no text for the current book is stepped
  over, so the Hebrew gives way to the Greek as you scroll from the Old
  Testament into the New.

Rungs are hidden in the continuous and chapter modes, whose point is unbroken
text, and return with the verse modes. The depth you opened is kept in the
shareable link.

### Cross-references

Each Bible column has a **✝** toggle in its header. With it on, a verse that has
cross-references carries a small ✝ badge with a count. Clicking the badge lists
the related passages — the ten best supported first, the rest one click away —
each with a line of its text in the edition you are reading. Any passage, or all
of them at once, opens in a new column beside the one you are in.

The references are OpenBible.info's set (CC BY 4.0): about 340,000 links, mostly
from the public-domain *Treasury of Scripture Knowledge*, ranked by reader votes.
They cover the Protestant canon; Quran, Hadith and Latter-day Saint columns have
none, and the toggle is greyed out there. Markers are off by default so the text
stays clean until you ask for them.

### Strong's numbers and word study

Strong's numbers give every Hebrew and Greek word of the Bible a stable number,
so a word can be followed into a lexicon and across the whole text without
reading the language. The **ⓝ** toggle in a column header switches them on.

- **In any Bible**, it opens the lineage rungs down to the original-language
  text and places a small number beside each Hebrew (`H430`) or Greek (`G2424`)
  word. The translation itself is not tagged: the numbers sit on the original
  beneath it, which is why every edition gains them at once. The Hebrew and
  Greek columns carry the numbers on their own text.
- **Hover, focus or tap a number** to see the word as written, its
  transliteration, a short gloss and a definition.
- **Double-click a number** for the full entry: the whole definition, how often
  the word occurs, and the passages that use it. A passage expands in place with
  the word highlighted, and beneath it the same verse in the Bible you are
  reading.

What to know before relying on it:

- **Sources.** Hebrew numbers come from the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible, Greek
  ones from STEPBible's tagging of the Textus Receptus (both CC BY 4.0). They
  cover 99.8% of Old Testament verses and 99.2% of New Testament words. Where
  the tagged source and the displayed text do not match exactly, the word is
  left unnumbered rather than guessed.
- **Translations of the lexicon.** Glosses and definitions exist in English for
  all 8,674 Hebrew and 5,523 Greek entries. In the other interface languages
  they are machine-translated, most frequent words first, and labelled as
  unreviewed; an entry not yet translated is shown in English.
- **Verse numbering.** Where your Bible numbers a verse differently from the
  Hebrew, the translation line says where the verse was found. Where the mapping
  is uncertain, no line is shown rather than a wrong one.
- **Age.** Strong's definitions date from 1890. Treat them as pointers into a
  lexicon, not as the last word on a word.

### Shareable links

The reader's full state — each column's text, the passage it is open at, its
display mode and reading order, any translation overlay, and whether the columns
scroll together — is captured in the page URL. Copy the link to return to the
exact same view later, or share it with someone else. References are edition- and
language-independent, so a link created in one translation opens correctly in
another.

This is a best-effort push to get public-domain scripture into audio at all, not
a published audio Bible. The reading is synthetic — neural text-to-speech, one
voice per edition, no human narrator and no studio pass — and it is generated a
few chapters a night inside a free character allowance, so a Bible-sized edition
takes months to fill in. Only public-domain texts are read; editions whose
licence forbids derivative works are excluded on purpose.

The point is to cover texts that have nothing. Finnish 1933 and the Delitzsch
Hebrew New Testament have no audio edition worth the name, while English is
served abundantly elsewhere — which is why English is deliberately last in the
queue rather than first.

### What the machine reading gets wrong

Worth knowing before listening, and worth reporting when you hear it:

- **Proper names.** Hebrew and Greek names are frequently mispronounced, in
  every language read here. The verse text goes to the engine as plain running
  text with no pronunciation lexicon, so nothing corrects a name it guesses
  wrong.
- **Archaic spelling.** The 1776 Finnish predates modern orthography and the
  engine's text normaliser was not built for it. Numbers, abbreviations and
  old spellings are read however it reads them.
- **One voice throughout.** Narration, dialogue and quoted speech share the same
  register; the audio never marks who is speaking.
- **Verse timings can be imperfect.** Chapters are synthesised as continuous
  prose on purpose, so phrasing carries across verse divisions instead of
  stopping at every one. The offsets come from marks the engine reports as it
  passes each verse and are normally exact — but a chapter where it reports
  fewer marks than the chapter has verses is still published, with the shortfall
  only noted in the run log, so a few chapters may highlight imprecisely.
- **Seams in long chapters.** Anything past roughly 7,500 characters is
  synthesised in parts and rejoined. Joins are placed at verse boundaries, where
  a pause is natural, but one can occasionally be heard.

Nothing already generated is re-generated automatically — a chapter on disk is a
chapter already paid for — so correcting a bad reading is a deliberate act:
delete that chapter and let the next night's run rebuild it.

## Commentary and arguments

Beyond the primary texts, the platform ingests and indexes argument material
about specific verses, drawn from a range of perspectives across the Abrahamic
traditions and from critical analysis. These are linked to the verses they
discuss, so a reader studying a contested passage can see how it is argued from
different angles.

## What a free account adds

Reading never requires an account. Every text, column, display mode, lineage
rung, cross-reference, Strong's number, search and public comment is open to
everyone. An account is free, and it adds only what an anonymous visitor cannot
have.

**A private study layer**

- **Comments and notes on any verse.** Click a verse number to open the editor:
  your text, the passages it cites (as many as you like, e.g. a theme traced
  across several books) and an optional video link. Everything is private until
  you choose to publish, and your own annotations — drafts included — appear
  under every verse they cite, in every edition.
- **Highlights.** Tint a verse in one of five colours. A highlight belongs to
  the verse, not to an edition, so it shows in every translation and column.
- **Saved searches.** Star a query in the search dialog to keep it and run it
  again later.
- **Preferences.** Your default Bible, display mode, reading order and interface
  language; the reader's look; whether comment and cross-reference markers show
  from the start; and "continue where I left off", which reopens the reader at
  your last position.
- **Muted commenters.** Silence any voice — an imported channel or another
  reader — and it stays hidden in the text and in the comments panel.

**A way to contribute**

- **Publish a comment.** Publishing is a separate, deliberate step, open to
  accounts with contributor standing. Nothing private becomes public by
  accident.
- **Curate your own channel.** Channels that claim their account can correct
  and curate their own imported arguments directly.
- **Web API keys.** Up to five keys for the programmatic API, managed from the
  profile page.

**Yours to take and to remove**

- **Download your data** as one file: profile, preferences, notes, highlights,
  saved searches and comments.
- **Change your email or password**, each confirmed with your current password.
- **Delete your account** permanently, with everything it owns, public comments
  included.

Private stays private: your notes, highlights and saved searches are never shown
to another reader and never enter the public search.
